# Decision memo — LLM access and cost for Panoscope

**To:** the author · **Date:** 2026-09-11 · **Status:** decision-ready; §7 lists what must be re-checked before submission
**In-repo facts verified today:** `agent/loop.py:826` is the *only* LLM client construction in the repo (`anthropic.Anthropic(api_key=self._api_key)`, key from `ANTHROPIC_API_KEY` at `:800`); `agent/config.py:53` pins `PRIMARY_MODEL = os.getenv("PANOSCOPE_MODEL", "claude-sonnet-4-6")`; a repo-wide grep for `cache_control` and `base_url` across all `.py` returns **zero matches**; `requirements.txt` lists `anthropic` and no other provider SDK.

## 1. The bottom line

**No third-party application may draw inference from a user's consumer subscription — not Claude Pro/Max, not ChatGPT Plus/Pro/Go, not Google AI Pro/Ultra — and this is not a grey area.** Anthropic's Consumer Terms permit automated access to the Services "[e]xcept when you are accessing our Services via an Anthropic API Key or where we otherwise explicitly permit it," forbid sharing Account credentials, and Anthropic's Claude Code legal page states flatly that "Anthropic does not permit third-party developers to offer Claude.ai login into their own applications, or to route requests through Free, Pro, or Max plan credentials on behalf of their users… developers may not collect, store, or intermediate Claude.ai credentials or session tokens." OpenAI's consumer Terms of Use independently prohibit programmatic extraction of Output, credential sharing, and circumventing rate limits, and its help centre glosses this as barring "[r]eselling access or using ChatGPT to power third-party services." Google publishes no clause squarely on point, so the honest verdict there is *unsanctioned and undocumented* rather than *expressly prohibited* — but there is no supported mechanism either, which makes the distinction academic. The one path that *is* legitimate, and is the path you have already chosen, inverts the direction of control: you ship instructions and code that the biologist runs **inside their own first-party client under their own credential**, where you never touch the credential and never resell the quota. For the Streamlit app, the only lawful fuels are an API key (the user's own, or an institutional/cloud endpoint), or a local open-weight model. Design for those three and stop treating subscription auth as an option that was foreclosed by cost; it was foreclosed by contract.

## 2. Per provider: permitted, prohibited, and merely unsupported

I keep these three categories separate deliberately. Conflating "not included in the plan" with "prohibited by the terms" is the commonest defect in write-ups of this question, and a referee will catch it.

### Anthropic

**Prohibited.**
- Consumer Terms (effective 8 Oct 2025), Prohibited Uses §7: *"Except when you are accessing our Services via an Anthropic API Key or where we otherwise explicitly permit it, to access the Services through automated or non-human means, whether through a bot, script, or otherwise."*
- Consumer Terms, account security: *"You may not share your Account login information, Anthropic API key, or Account credentials with anyone else. You also may not make your Account available to anyone else."*
- Consumer Terms §3(2): *"To develop any products or services that compete with our Services, including … resell the Services."*
- **The single best citation, and the one the earlier research missed:** `code.claude.com/docs/en/legal-and-compliance` — *"Anthropic does not permit third-party developers to offer Claude.ai login into their own applications, or to route requests through Free, Pro, or Max plan credentials on behalf of their users. Moreover, developers may not collect, store, or intermediate Claude.ai credentials or session tokens — sign-in to a Claude account must complete through Anthropic's own flow."* Cite this in preference to the Agent SDK note; it is first-party, explicit, and names the exact mechanism.
- Agent SDK overview and quickstart, verbatim: *"Unless previously approved, Anthropic does not allow third party developers to offer claude.ai login or rate limits for their products, including agents built on the Claude Agent SDK."*
- Commercial Terms D.4: no accessing the Services to build a competing product or resell them "except as expressly approved by Anthropic."
- Branding: "Claude Code" and "Claude Code Agent" are not permitted product names; "Claude Agent" or "{YourName} Powered by Claude" are.
- Naming: a Skill `name` "[c]annot contain reserved words: 'anthropic', 'claude'"; 17 marketplace names are reserved, and "[n]ames that impersonate official marketplaces, such as `official-claude-plugins` or `anthropic-plugins-v2`, are also blocked."

**Permitted.**
- Publishing a skill/plugin the user installs into their own Claude Code, run under their own credential. Three documented routes: commit `.claude/skills/` to version control; ship a `skills/` directory inside a plugin; deploy via managed settings org-wide.
- Hosting your own plugin marketplace (a git repo with `.claude-plugin/marketplace.json`) on GitHub, GitLab, Bitbucket, self-hosted git, a private repo, or any static file server. No Anthropic approval is documented as required — though note that is an argument from silence, not a stated permission.
- Submitting to `anthropics/claude-plugins-community` via `platform.claude.com/plugins/submit` (individual authors) or the claude.ai admin form (Team/Enterprise orgs), subject to `claude plugin validate` plus automated safety screening, pinned to a commit SHA.
- `claude setup-token` — a one-year OAuth token — **for the subscriber's own CI and scripts only**. This is not a distribution mechanism.
- Shipping or hosting the **unmodified Claude Code binary** in your product where each end user signs in themselves through Anthropic's flow, under Commercial Terms, with no reselling or intermediating. Worth knowing; almost certainly not what you want.
- Agent SDK embedded in a distributed product **billed to your own API account**: Commercial Terms A.1 — *"Anthropic gives Customer permission to use the Services, including to power products and services Customer makes available to its own customers and end users."*

**Merely unsupported / not included (do not call these prohibitions).**
- Claude Code is not included on the Free plan (the pricing table's Free row reads "Claude Code: No"). That is an entitlement limit, not a breach.
- API access is not part of Pro/Max/Team. **Careful with the sourcing:** `claude.com/pricing` nowhere states this; it is inferred from silence there. Cite instead the Pro/Max support article — API usage "will be billed at standard API rates (distinct from Pro/Max Plan pricing)" — together with the Consumer/Commercial split, which the Claude Code legal page states as the licence terms for Claude Code.
- Pro/Max usage limits are shared across Claude and Claude Code. **This is in flux.** Anthropic announced, then on 15 June 2026 paused, a change that would have moved Agent SDK and `claude -p` usage onto separate monthly credits; the help centre currently says "For now, nothing has changed" and that "Claude Agent SDK, `claude -p`, and third-party app usage still draw from your subscription's usage limits." Re-read before you submit.
- The "previously approved" exception has **no self-serve application form**, but it does have a documented contact path: the legal-and-compliance page says *"For questions about permitted authentication methods for your use case, please contact sales."* Earlier research reported this as a dead end; that was half wrong.
- If you distribute a **standalone MCP server** through Anthropic's directory, the **Anthropic Software Directory Policy (15 April 2026)** governs it and has a dedicated §5 for MCP servers (graceful errors, token frugality, 64-char tool names, OAuth 2.0 with recognised-CA certificates for remote servers, `readOnlyHint`/`destructiveHint`/`title` annotations, Streamable HTTP), plus developer requirements (privacy-policy link, verified contact, test account, three working examples). Repo-install and self-hosted marketplace distribution avoid it entirely.
- The Usage Policy's cross-account clause is narrower than it is often quoted: it bars coordinating **malicious** activity across accounts. Do not drop the qualifier.
- Anthropic's Service Specific Terms contain nothing about Claude Code, the Agent SDK, or subscriptions. That absence means the Consumer/Commercial Terms plus the legal-and-compliance page govern — not that a gap exists.

### OpenAI

**Prohibited.**
- Terms of Use (rest-of-world variant, effective 1 Jan 2026; the Europe variant, updated 16 Jan 2026, is worded in the gerund — **pin the variant and date, the canonical URL is geo-routed**): *"Automatically or programmatically extract data or Output"*; *"You may not share your account credentials or make your account available to anyone else"*; *"Interfere with or disrupt our Services, including circumvent any rate limits or restrictions"*; *"Modify, copy, lease, sell or distribute any of our Services."*
- Services Agreement §3.1: *"Customer may not resell or lease access to its Account or any End User Account."* §3.3(g): no *"buy, sell, or transfer API keys from, to, or with a third party."*
- Plugin guidelines: commerce is limited to physical goods ("[s]elling digital products or services—including subscriptions, digital content, tokens, or credits—is not allowed"); no "unofficial connectors… including pass-through intermediary software layers"; and **no Protected Health Information or payment-card data** (also App Developer Terms §2.4). For a biomedical tool this is the load-bearing constraint: de-identified Xenium demo data is fine, patient-identifiable data cannot live in a ChatGPT plugin.

**Permitted.**
- Publishing a plugin (skills + MCP server + optional UI) into the universal ChatGPT/Codex directory. *"Plugins are the packages people discover, install, share, and publish in ChatGPT and Codex."* Your MCP server runs on your infrastructure; inference runs on the user's plan. §7.3: *"you do so as an independent developer using OpenAI's Services as a platform, and not as an OpenAI customer."*
- A plugin may let a user *"sign in to an existing paid account and access features already included in their subscription"* — the clean bring-your-own-account pattern, subject to no plan display, no checkout links, no upgrade promotion.
- Codex under a ChatGPT plan ("Codex is included across ChatGPT plans, including Free and Go. Usage limits vary by plan."), or with your own API key.
- **Correction to a widely repeated misreading:** the sentence that ChatGPT "does **not** support machine-to-machine OAuth grants such as client credentials, service accounts, or JWT bearer assertions" is about ChatGPT's *outbound* authentication to **your** MCP server, not about whether anything can drive ChatGPT. Restated correctly: your MCP server cannot authenticate ChatGPT with an API key or client-credentials grant; use OpenAI-managed mTLS or egress-IP allowlisting plus user-level OAuth 2.1.

**Merely unsupported / not included.**
- API usage is excluded from Plus and Go ("API usage is separate and billed independently"). Help-centre pages, frequently edited — date-stamp any citation.
- OpenAI may *"reject or remove an App at any time and for any reason"* (§7.5) and guarantees no placement or ranking (§7.4).
- GPTs: *"They are not a way to embed ChatGPT in an external website or application. To build an assistant into a product, use the API."* New GPT creation is unavailable on personal accounts and has moved to managed workspaces.
- **Workspace Agents API** is the one real programmatic door into a plan — `POST https://api.chatgpt.com/v1/workspace_agents/{id}/trigger`, admin-enabled and user-created token, workspace plans only — and it is useless as an inference channel: *"The agent's response cannot currently be retrieved through the API."* A beta run-status endpoint now returns queued/in_progress/completed, status only.
- No free API tier. Complimentary daily tokens exist for organisations that **opt into sharing prompts and completions for training**, require a positive balance, are not universally offered, and are structurally unavailable to accounts with Zero Data Retention — i.e. the posture a medical research institute is most likely to have.

### Google

**Prohibited.**
- Gemini API Additional Terms (effective 23 Mar 2026): *"You may use only Paid Services when making API Clients available to users in the European Economic Area, Switzerland, or the United Kingdom."* This is the sharpest constraint in the whole set for a Nature Methods paper — a demo shipped with a free-tier key that a European reader can invoke violates it, and the obligation attaches to where your users are, not where you are.
- Same terms: no developing competing models, no reverse-engineering, extracting or replicating model components including parameter weights. Not engaged by Panoscope's design, but it *does* bar distilling Gemini output into a local model, if that ever occurs to you as a fallback strategy.
- Google Terms of Service (effective 30 Jul 2026): no *"using automated means to access content from any of our services in violation of the machine-readable instructions on our web pages"*; no *"providing services that appear to originate from you (or someone else) when they actually originate from us."* Note the automation clause is scoped to violating machine-readable instructions, not a blanket ban.

**Permitted.**
- Paid Gemini API: *"Google doesn't use your prompts (including associated system instructions, cached content, and files…) or responses to improve our products."* This is the only defensible Gemini configuration for a tool that may see unpublished data.
- The Gemini CLI extension ecosystem — third-party extensions installed by repository URL, overwhelmingly `@owner/repo` GitHub projects, with no review gate.

**Merely unsupported.**
- Consuming a consumer Gemini subscription programmatically. No clause names it; no mechanism supports it.
- Google AI Pro/Ultra raise limits in Google's **own first-party developer tools** (Jules, Antigravity — Pro gets *entry* limits, only Ultra gets raised ones). These are entitlements to Google clients, not API capacity, and cannot back a third-party tool. **Correct the earlier "unlike OpenAI" contrast — it is false**; Codex is likewise included across ChatGPT plans.
- The Gemini API **unpaid tier is disqualifying on data grounds, not permission grounds**: *"Google uses the content you submit to the Services and any generated responses to provide, improve, and develop Google products and services and machine learning technologies."*

## 3. The skill-in-their-own-client path

**It holds up. It is the cleanest compliance position available to you, and it is better documented than any alternative.** The reasoning is short: you distribute instructions and code that execute on the user's machine; the user authenticates themselves, to Anthropic, in a first-party client, with their own credential; you never collect, store, or intermediate that credential; you resell nothing. None of Consumer Terms §2, §3(2) or §3(7) is engaged, and Anthropic documents three distribution routes plus two marketplaces for exactly this. Shipping `skills/marker-interpretation/SKILL.md` and `skills/enrichment-interpretation/SKILL.md` as an MIT repo is the canonical supported pattern, and CLAUDE.md's existing requirement that the skill "must install and run standalone, independent of the app" already aligns with it. Ship it.

Seven caveats, in descending order of how much they should change your design:

1. **The skill path does not carry the grounding guarantee, and the paper must say so.** In the biologist's own client there is no `agent/verdict.py` and no `agent/grounding_check.py` running — there is a prompt asking a model to behave. The abstention literature is unambiguous that this is not enough: AbstentionBench finds *"abstention is an unsolved problem, and one where scaling models is of little use"* and that *"a carefully crafted system prompt can boost abstention in practice, [but] it does not resolve models' fundamental inability to reason about uncertainty"*; RefusalBench finds refusal accuracy below 50% on multi-document tasks with *"neither scale nor extended reasoning"* helping. So: ship the grounding checker as an executable script inside the skill folder, instruct the agent to run it, and — decisively — make the **study artifact** carry the checker's verdicts so that anything produced in the client is re-validated when it enters the app. The artifact-as-contract decision is what makes the two paths reconcilable; make it carry provenance (provider, route of access, model id, `queried_at`, whether the grounding gate ran and with what result), or the two paths emit indistinguishable outputs of very different trustworthiness.
2. **Name constraints bite.** A Skill `name` cannot contain "claude" or "anthropic"; 17 marketplace names are reserved and impersonating names are blocked; if you ever wrap the Agent SDK, you may not call the product "Claude Code."
3. **If you distribute an MCP server through Anthropic's directory**, the Software Directory Policy §5 applies in full. Repo-install or your own marketplace avoids it. Pick deliberately.
4. **Institutional users may not be able to install it.** Organisations "can restrict which marketplaces users are allowed to add using managed marketplace restrictions." Document the plain-git install path as the fallback.
5. **The OpenAI plugin variant, if you ever build it, carries a hard PHI ban and removal-at-will.** Fine for the de-identified demo; not a home for patient data; and OpenAI can delist you for any reason.
6. **Do not cite the Agent Skills security page as your permission.** It says *"Use Skills only from trusted sources: those you created yourself or obtained from Anthropic"* — read literally, that is narrower than the marketplace it sits beside. Cite the Claude Code skills, plugins and plugin-marketplaces docs, which are explicit and permissive.
7. **Anthropic disclaims verification** ("Anthropic doesn't control what MCP servers, files, or other software are included in plugins and can't verify that they work as intended") and plugins "execute arbitrary code on your machine with your user privileges." That is fine — it is the same posture as any Bioconductor package — but it means your grounding tests, not a marketplace badge, are the trust mechanism. Say that in the paper.

## 4. What intake actually costs, and whether the premise survives

**The token model.** The expense is not the 100K context ceiling; it is that the API is stateless, so every round resends the whole history. With `S` = stable system prefix (system prompt + skill contract + tool schemas ≈ 8,000), `U` = study context ≈ 1,500, `T` = mean tool result appended per round ≈ 2,500, `O` = mean model output per round ≈ 700, `N` = rounds ≈ 30, `F` = final structured emission ≈ 10,000:

```
uncached input = N(S+U) + (T+O)·N(N−1)/2
               = 30(9,500) + 3,200·435 = 285,000 + 1,392,000 = 1,677,000
output         = N·O + F = 31,000
peak context   = S + U + N(T+O) = 105,500
```

Uncached input is **quadratic in rounds**, not linear in peak context — the peak (105,500) understates the billed input (1,677,000) by ~16×. That identity is exact, and it is the genuinely non-obvious, publishable observation in this whole analysis. State it in the paper.

**The money.** At Anthropic's current rates: Sonnet 5 ($2/$10 per MTok) → 1.677 × 2 + 0.031 × 10 = **$3.66 raw**, ≈ **$0.88 with incremental caching**. Opus 5 ($5/$25) → **$9.16 raw, ≈$2.20 cached**. Gemini 3.8 Flash lands near **$0.31** cached; a frontier OpenAI tier near **$4.4**. Add ~30% to token counts on Claude 4.7-and-later models (newer tokenizer) when comparing like for like.

Caching buys **75–82%**, not the headline 90%, because it discounts only the input term. Decomposing the Opus 5 case: cache write 29%, cache read 36%, **output 35%** — three roughly equal thirds. The corollary matters: after caching, further input optimisation has sharply diminishing returns, and the lever that remains is output verbosity and per-round thinking.

**Publish a range, not point estimates.** Two figures in the working notes are mutually inconsistent — $4.40/study for the frontier OpenAI tier versus $46.03 for ten studies (implying $4.60), and Sonnet 5's $0.88/study annualises to $8.80, below the "$10–14 mid-tier" band asserted alongside it. Reconcile or, better, replace with measurement. The defensible statement today is: **$0.3–$4.5 per study with caching across mid-to-frontier tiers; whole envelope $0.14–$7.7; a ten-study lab-year in the single digits to low tens of dollars.**

**Comparators.** GPTCelltype (*Nat Methods* 2024): *"does not exceed $0.1 for all queries in this study."* CASSIA (*Nat Commun* 2025): ~$0.02/annotation on GPT-4o, $0.03 on Claude 3.5 Sonnet, $0.003 on LLaMA-3.2-90B. mLLMCelltype: $0.10–$1.00 per 10–20-cluster dataset single-model. scExtract (*Genome Biology* 2025): *"at a cost of less than one dollar"* per dataset, with providers capped at "≤ $5.00 per 1M input tokens" — note that earlier research wrongly recorded scExtract as reporting no cost; it reports one, and it corroborates your number at the same order of magnitude. Use it.

**Does the "most expensive step" premise survive?** **Relatively yes; absolutely no.** Intake is ~50–200× a single chat turn, 5–25× the published per-dataset norm, and larger than Panoscope's entire existing 99-call Tier-B notes pipeline. It is genuinely the dominant line item, and the quadratic structure is worth a paragraph in the Methods. But the number being defended is **one to four dollars**. No authentication or billing layer recovers its own first day of engineering time against that, and building one would contradict CLAUDE.md's own "do not add breadth before the one marker path is solid end to end." The premise justifies *instrumentation and a cost table*, not plumbing.

**The two things that actually move the number are both in this repo and both unbuilt.** There is no prompt caching anywhere (`cache_control`: zero matches), which on the currently pinned model is the difference between $5.50 and $1.32 per intake — worth roughly 4× more than any provider switch. And `PRIMARY_MODEL` defaults to `claude-sonnet-4-6` at $3/$15 against Sonnet 5's $2/$10, i.e. 50% more per token on both directions for an older model (the pin is env-overridable, so this is a default, not a binding). Do caching first, then the default bump, then cap tool-result size (the gap between trimmed and raw literature payloads is ~2× and, on Gemini 3.1 Pro, crosses the >200k price step), then emit a per-intake cost receipt from `response.usage` so the paper's cost claim is **measured rather than modelled**. That receipt is about twenty lines and it converts your weakest number into your strongest.

One housekeeping note: if the "0.05–0.1% of a Xenium slide / 3–5 minutes of a postdoc's time" framing survives into the manuscript, source both denominators to a real core-facility price list and a real salary scale, or soften to an order-of-magnitude statement. Both are currently unsourced assumptions.

## 5. Recommended access architecture

The core insight: **you do not need a router, you need one configurable client and one adapter.** Every path below is reachable through either (a) the Anthropic SDK with a `base_url` and an auth backend, or (b) the OpenAI SDK with `base_url` + `api_key` — and (b) alone covers Azure's v1 endpoint, vLLM, Ollama, a LiteLLM proxy, and OpenRouter. Two code paths, not twelve. The repo is unusually well placed for this: there is exactly one client construction to change.

**P0 — keep the Anthropic default; make it configurable; ship caching.** The SDK already reads `ANTHROPIC_BASE_URL` from the environment (precedence: kwarg > `ANTHROPIC_BASE_URL` > profile config > hardcoded default), so an institutional gateway becomes almost free at `agent/loop.py:826`. Add prompt caching in the same change — 1h TTL for the human-paced elicitation phase (Anthropic's 5m TTL will miss while a biologist is typing), 5m for autonomous back-to-back rounds. Add the `usage` receipt.

**P1 — one OpenAI-compatible adapter behind `PANOSCOPE_PROVIDER`** (`anthropic | openai_compatible | vertex | bedrock | local`). Three documentation landmines to call out explicitly in the README, because each returns a confusing error rather than a clear one:
- **Azure**: the `model` field must be *your deployment name*, not the model name. This is the single most common porting bug.
- **Bedrock**: the cross-region-inference generation cannot be invoked by bare model id — you need a region-prefixed inference profile (`us.` / `eu.` / `jp.` / `apac.` / `global.`) or a full ARN, else HTTP 400. The newest models (Opus 5, Sonnet 5, Fable 5, Opus 4.8/4.7) are reachable through `InvokeModel` without ARN-versioned ids, so "newer models require a profile" is imprecise — say "the cross-region-inference generation."
- **Vertex/Agent Platform**: the model goes in the endpoint URL, not the body, and the body must carry `anthropic_version: "vertex-2023-10-16"`. Ship Vertex and Bedrock as extras (`panoscope[vertex]`, `panoscope[bedrock]`).
- Delegate Entra ID / ADC / SigV4 to each SDK's default credential chain. **Never ask a user to paste a cloud credential into `.env`.**

**P2 — local, through the same adapter.** Ollama at `http://localhost:11434/v1` and vLLM at `http://localhost:8000/v1` are the *same code path* as P1 — which is the whole argument for building P1. Three specifics:
- **Emit Tier-B notes under a JSON Schema using the backend's constrained decoding** (Ollama's `format` parameter; vLLM's `structured_outputs` with `xgrammar`/`guidance`). Note vLLM removed `guided_json`/`guided_regex`/`guided_decoding_backend` in v0.12.0 — any tutorial older than that produces broken code. Constrained decoding removes an entire class of parse failures and makes a mid-size local model viable: the grounding gate then only has to verify *truth*, not *shape*.
- **Disable thinking mode for the grounded extraction step.** AbstentionBench: reasoning fine-tuning degrades abstention by 24% on average, which is precisely your failure mode.
- **Model class**: the Qwen3.5/3.8 generation, 27B dense or 35B-A3B MoE, Apache-2.0. Hardware floor 32 GB unified / 24 GB VRAM at Q4; 48–64 GB for comfort at long context. The MoE variant is the laptop default — ~58 t/s on an M3 Max against ~17 t/s for a dense 27–32B, because this workload is memory-bandwidth-bound. **But do not pick by leaderboard rank**: Vectara's HHEM puts `qwen3.5-27b` at 12.1% hallucination against `qwen3-8b` at 4.8% — newest and most capable is not most faithful. Choose on your own calibration set, which CLAUDE.md already obliges you to keep. Ollama, llama.cpp (both MIT) and vLLM (Apache-2.0) are all clean for an MIT tool.

**P3 — no model at all.** Tier A (`agent/verdict.py`) plus `agent/fallback.py` already produce a complete, grounded, citable answer for every cluster with no network. Say so in the README, in one sentence: **"Panoscope runs with no LLM; the literature layer degrades, the numbers do not."** Nobody else in this field makes that claim, and it is true.

**Against the four users:**

- **(a) Has an API key.** Unchanged, zero friction. The highest-value single action in this memo: apply to **Anthropic's AI for Science programme — up to $50,000 in credits per project, any researcher eligible, explicit life-sciences focus.** It is a form, not a procurement. Two things the earlier research blurred: the separate "Claude team plan for scientists" (10,000 seats for PIs at academic or nonprofit research institutions) is a *subscription*, not credits, and therefore cannot power the tool; and biology/chemistry researchers under that programme "will still be limited to our Opus-class models." OpenAI's Researcher Access Program is ~$1,000. OpenAI's ChatGPT for Academic Researchers is chat-only — *"The offer covers ChatGPT access and does not include OpenAI API credits"* — and is irrelevant here.
- **(b) Has only an institutional endpoint.** P1 adapter. Set expectations honestly in the docs: most 2026 institutional AI provision in Australia is a **chat licence** (Copilot, ChatGPT Edu — UNSW deployed to 80,000 staff and students in September 2026), not a programmatic endpoint, and a researcher with "institutional AI access" will typically need an IT ticket to get an Azure OpenAI deployment. Design for the ones who succeed; do not assume it.
- **(c) Has only local hardware.** P2, then P3. ARDC's Nectar Research Cloud offers "free or subsidised access to high-performance computing resources, including GPUs" and ships a pre-configured "Generative AI and Large Language Models (LLMs)" environment — a credible hosting target, though a Nectar allocation is required. On WEHI Milton specifically: **be careful how you phrase this**. A Slurm + Ollama recipe exists under a WEHI-ResearchComputing repo (`sbatch ollama-submit.sh`, server on `gpu-a100-n01`, models cached to `/vast/scratch`), but that repo self-describes as a 2024 RAG toy example copied from a tutorial, with zero stars and hardcoded demo credentials. It evidences that a recipe exists, **not** that Research Computing operates Ollama as a supported service. Confirm before writing it into a paper. The `gpuq` per-user ceiling (8 × A30 and 1 × A100 across 2 nodes, 48-hour walltime) comes from 2025 workshop material and is indicative only. If it is real, Panoscope needs one line: `PANOSCOPE_BASE_URL=http://gpu-a100-n01:11434/v1`.
- **(d) Reviewer reproducing in three years.** The closed model will not be there. Anthropic's own deprecation page: `claude-3-7-sonnet` retired 2026-02-19; `claude-sonnet-4` and `claude-opus-4` 2026-06-15; `claude-opus-4-1` 2026-08-05 — an observed ~12–13-month release-to-retirement cycle, with "at least 60 days' notice," the explicit acknowledgement that *"Researchers lose access to models for ongoing and comparative studies,"* a **commitment** to long-term weight preservation but only a **hope** of future public availability. (Your pinned `claude-sonnet-4-6` is Active, retirement not sooner than 2027-02-17; Bedrock and Vertex set their own schedules.) So the reproducibility tier is **open weights plus determinism plus frozen artifacts**: extend `manifest.json` — which already carries provenance and artifact sha256s — with an inference-provenance block (provider, route of access, model id, `queried_at`, sampling params, constrained-decoding schema; for open weights the HF repo + revision SHA + GGUF quant + file sha256, plus runtime name and version such as `llama.cpp b9020` or `vllm 0.12.x`); keep shipping the frozen `interp/gene_notes.json` and `celltype_notes.json` so a reviewer reproduces the *published* interpretation byte-for-byte and can separately re-run the live layer to observe drift.

**What not to do.** Do not make LiteLLM or OpenRouter a required dependency. LiteLLM's root is MIT and safe to `pip install` and import, but everything under `enterprise/` is commercial — *"it is forbidden to copy, merge, publish, distribute, sublicense, and/or sell the Software"* — so never vendor from it and never document an enterprise-gated feature as part of Panoscope. OpenRouter is excellent to *document* and poor to *depend on*: it is a third-party account with binding arbitration and a class-action waiver, a BYOK percentage fee above a free allowance, BYOK endpoints always tried first with "no way to change this behavior," and — fatally for a three-year reproducibility claim — *"OpenRouter may add or remove Models from the Service at any time."* Do not build the primary path on Anthropic's OpenAI-compatibility layer; their docs disclaim it as *"not considered a long-term or production-ready solution."* And do not let the model decide `verify` — three independent 2026 benchmarks say it cannot.

**Two data-governance rules are hard constraints, not preferences.** Google's unpaid tier trains on submitted content; OpenAI's complimentary tokens require opting into data sharing and are structurally unavailable to ZDR accounts. Neither is usable for unpublished lab data. Both should be named as unusable in the README rather than listed as free options.

## 6. What to write in the paper

**Methods — model disclosure.** Name the specific models, including proprietary ones, in the abstract and introduction. The *Nature Machine Intelligence* editorial asks exactly this — *"Rather than stating 'an' or 'the' LLM is part of the workflow or framework, it should be clear, already in the abstract and introduction, which models have been used, including proprietary ones"* — and asks for *"which exact version has been accessed (such as 'gpt-4-0613'…), with the date of access also included."* Phrase your citation as "a *Nature Machine Intelligence* editorial asks authors to…", not "policy requires"; it is an editorial with a "should," and a referee will notice the difference. TRIPOD-LLM item 6a wants name, version and last date of training (note plainly that last-training-date is unobtainable for closed models — that unmeetability is itself an argument). CHART items 3a/3b/6a/6b want version and release date, **open- versus closed-source**, **route of access**, and query dates. Every one of these is satisfied by the `manifest.json` inference-provenance block above, which makes your Tier A / Tier B split legible: Tier A needs none of it; Tier B carries all of it.

**Methods — justify the closed model explicitly.** Palmer, Smith & Spirling (*Nat Comput Sci* 4:2–3): *"we ask that scientists explicitly justify their use of proprietary models."* Your justification is their item (6), downstream use only — *"the LM itself… is not under study, and is being used as an intermediary for some other purpose. Furthermore, any data being produced by the LM is available for others to inspect."* Your grounding gate makes this argument unusually strong: model output is filtered by deterministic code and every surviving number traces to jazzPanda's output, the panel list, or a cited lab note. But do not cite item (6) as a free pass — the same paper's recommendations still open with *"Individuals ideally would not use closed LMs. If they do, they should be explicit about why… Preferably they would clarify what open models they compared their results to."*

**Results — ship the open-weight arm and report it honestly.** *NMI*: *"Authors are therefore strongly encouraged to include results with other, ideally open-source LLMs in their framework… authors can anticipate how the demonstrated framework might be implemented if the original model is no longer available."* Seven of thirteen comparable tools now ship a documented local path, so this is close to majority practice. Follow PaperQA2's precedent and be frank — *"You won't get good performance with 7B models"* is a published sentence in a well-regarded tool's README. Add an **open-model column to the calibration table** CLAUDE.md already requires. CASSIA validates `gpt-oss:20b` via Ollama for this exact job ("[g]ood for large bulk analysis with acceptable accuracy"), which gives you a citable starting point.

**Reproducibility statement — three tiers, stated as three tiers.** (i) Tier A is fully deterministic, requires no network and no model, and reproduces byte-for-byte from the committed, hashed inputs. (ii) The published Tier-B interpretation ships as a frozen artifact with full inference provenance. (iii) Live regeneration is model- and date-dependent; here is the open-weight configuration that reproduces it once the closed model is retired. That separation — frozen artifact versus live regeneration — is the honest answer to "is this reproducible?", it maps onto your existing tree with no redesign, and tier (i) is a claim essentially nobody else in this space can make.

**Data and code availability.** MIT, OSI-compliant, public repo — Genome Biology requires *"a public repository, with an OSI-compliant license"* and a declared "[a]ny restrictions to use by non-academics" (yours: none). Per-dataset tree with input hashes and the frozen interp artifacts. **State the reporting-summary position honestly**: the Nature Portfolio ML reporting summary makes you tick "[p]retrained models are used in the study and are not accessible" for the closed path; the open-weight arm lets you also tick the accessible box with a URL and a revision SHA. On paid APIs, Genome Biology's guidelines are **silent** — report that as silence plus the positive instance (scExtract, commercial-API-only, published there in 2025), not as a policy that permits.

**Cost and privacy paragraphs — cheap differentiators.** Only 3 of 13 comparable tools say anything concrete about money. Report per-study cost **measured** from `response.usage`, with the model, the caching configuration and the date. And copy cell-annotator's privacy disclosure pattern, which for Panoscope is even stronger than theirs: state exactly what leaves the machine — marker names, jazzPanda statistics, panel facts, tissue context, cited lab notes — and that **no expression matrix and no cell-level data are transmitted**.

**If a referee pushes back on the paid-API dependency**, the precedent paragraph writes itself: GPTCelltype (*Nat Methods* 2024, OpenAI-only, no local path, no cost statement), scExtract (*Genome Biology* 2025), GPTBioInsightor (*Bioinformatics Advances* 2026, nine commercial providers), CASSIA (*Nat Commun* 2025), and SpatialAgent (Genentech, MIT, Anthropic + OpenAI keys, no local path) — your nearest spatial analogue, which has not solved this either. A commercial API key is not a documented bar at any of these venues.

**Venue note.** The one place it *is* disqualifying is the **NAR Web Server Issue**, whose 2025 editorial reports that *"none of those that employ an LLM to interact with the user made it into this Issue"*, attributes rejections partly to *"unreliable output or 'hallucinations'"*, and objects that *"the potential need for payment or at least credit card registration when using commercial cloud platforms… conflicts with our fundamental principle of providing free and open-access web servers."* That objection is scoped to a free, login-free public web server, not to a downloadable tool a lab runs with its own key — do not let it frighten you out of the right venue. If you ever want NAR: a container, no login, a local model default, MCP-mediated live lookup. Worth one sentence in your Discussion regardless: **the 2026 NAR editorial names real-time external querying via MCP as something that "might offer a path forward"** past LLM hallucination, and Panoscope's live-PubMed-only citation rule is already built that way (cite the editorial and MCPmed together, keeping the "might" intact). Note too that the 2026 issue relaxed the free-commercial-use rule and reinstated containerised stand-alone software.

**One thing not to write.** Do not claim that referees have objected to commercial API dependencies. That negative is under-searched: Genome Biology publishes scExtract's full peer-review history and *Nature Communications* publishes CASSIA's referee reports, and neither was read. Claim only that **editors and reporting guidelines** have set requirements, and cite them.
